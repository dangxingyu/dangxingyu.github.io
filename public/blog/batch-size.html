Deriving and testing scaling rules.

01

One optimizer. Different tasks. Different scaling rules.

216 rules in language modeling. 648 on CIFAR-5M. The best common prescription differs, and the language-model winner also changes with the target batch.

Retuning tells us which optimizer works best at a batch size. A scaling rule asks a harder question: can we carry a configuration tuned at one batch to another, without paying for that search again? We test this for MuonW–AdamW, which uses MuonW for hidden weight matrices and AdamW for the remaining, auxiliary parameters.

Write κ=B′B for the ratio of the scaled batch to the reference batch. Two common ways to derive a rule give different answers. Matching a continuous-time stochastic model suggests linear scaling of Muon’s learning rate and transporting momentum to preserve its decay per sample. Minimizing a convergence bound instead suggests square-root learning-rate scaling with fixed momentum. Both keep matrix weight decay fixed. Here ηM and λM are Muon’s learning rate and weight decay, and μ is its momentum coefficient. These are prescriptions under specified assumptions, rather than guarantees that an implemented optimizer’s final validation loss will stay unchanged.

We put these candidates inside a much wider search. Each learning-rate or weight-decay coordinate can stay fixed, grow as the square root of the batch ratio, or grow linearly. Each momentum or moment coefficient can stay fixed or preserve its decay over the data budget. With auxiliary weight decay fixed, that makes 216 complete rules in language modeling: three choices for each of three coordinates, and two choices for each of three coefficients. Scaling auxiliary weight decay separately gives 648 rules on CIFAR-5M.

All rules start from the same tuned reference configuration within each task. We evaluate four larger batches in language modeling and six target batches in one-pass CIFAR-5M training with a GroupNorm ResNet. To select one common rule for a task, we minimize the mean gap to the best tested rule at each batch, weighting target batches equally. A common rule can therefore be the best compromise without winning every batch.

Each curve is a complete rule, and each point is one original-grid run: 864 language-model runs or 3,888 CIFAR-5M runs, one seed per setting. Language-model loss is terminal validation cross-entropy after approximately 1.7B tokens; CIFAR-5M loss averages the final ten evaluations of one pass over 5M training images. Bound and SDE presets show the best mean-gap completion of each Muon prescription within the grid; the derivations alone do not specify every auxiliary AdamW choice. These losses use the original single-seed grids, separate from the paper’s repeated-run full-retuning comparison.

The best common language-model rule keeps both learning-rate groups fixed and scales matrix weight decay linearly. It keeps Muon momentum and Adam’s first-moment coefficient fixed while transporting the second-moment coefficient. Its mean loss gap is 0.00151 nats. CIFAR-5M instead favors square-root scaling of both learning rates, with both weight decays and all three coefficients fixed. The same optimizer favors different adjustments in the two tasks.

Even within the language model, the batch-specific winner changes: both learning rates scale with the square root at 256K and 512K, but stay fixed with linear matrix weight decay at 1M and 2M. The full-retuning comparison in the paper reinforces the task dependence: the bound-based rule stays within 0.006 nats of full retuning on CIFAR-5M, while both theoretical candidates fall behind no scaling in language modeling from 1M onward. The transfer search asks how to adjust one optimizer. Next we return to the more basic choice: whether the optimizer itself should change with batch size.

Optimizer rankings across batch sizes.

02

SOAP wins at 128K. Shampoo wins at 2M.

Independently retuned hyperparameters. The same 1.7 billion training-token budget. The reversal appears under both norm-control settings.

Suppose you have found an optimizer that trains your model well. You now want to increase the batch size to make better use of your hardware. It is tempting to treat this as a separate decision: keep the optimizer, adjust its learning rate, and continue. But a larger batch changes the gradients the optimizer receives. If the optimizer shapes its updates using those gradients, why should the same choice remain best?

We tested this question in language-model pretraining. We compared Adam, Lion, Muon, SOAP, and Shampoo at four batch sizes, from 128K to 2M tokens per update. Each optimizer received its own hyperparameter search at each batch. Every run processed roughly 1.7 billion training tokens. Under weight decay, SOAP’s validation loss is about 0.0113 nats lower than Shampoo’s at 128K. At 2M, Shampoo’s validation loss is about 0.0211 nats lower than SOAP’s. The winner also changes under HyperBall, which keeps each hidden matrix’s norm fixed instead of shrinking it with weight decay. Move the batch-size control below to follow the reversal.

Retuning matters here. A ranking reversal caused by copying a bad learning rate would tell us little about the optimizers. These crossovers survive extensive independent searches, although those searches do not certify globally optimal hyperparameters. The comparison concerns validation loss after a fixed token budget; it does not rank wall-clock training speed. Within that setting, the result asks us to reconsider something more basic: what makes rescaling the gradient useful when the gradient is noisy? To explore that question, we will replace the language model with a valley we can inspect.

Geometry and gradient noise.

03

Correcting curvature also rescales the noise.

A preconditioner changes both progress from the starting point and the noise left by stochastic updates. Batch size changes that tradeoff.

A sharp direction is one in which loss rises rapidly as you move away from the minimum. A flat direction changes loss more slowly. Our toy valley has one of each: the horizontal direction (w1) is flat and noisy, while the vertical direction (w2) is sharp and noiseless. A minibatch gradient estimates the slope using a finite set of samples. Increasing the batch averages away some of that noise. It also spends more samples on each update. With 4,096 samples available, batch 1 permits 4,096 updates; batch 256 permits only 16. Cleaner gradients come with fewer chances to move. The playground uses a quadratic valley with fixed, known curvatures and independent Gaussian noise at each update. The default starting point has both coordinates equal to 1; click the valley to move it.

SGD multiplies both gradient coordinates by the same learning rate. Newton’s method first divides each coordinate by its curvature, so the sharp direction no longer dictates how slowly the flat direction must move. This rescaling is called preconditioning. It acts on the whole estimated gradient, including its noise. A larger learning rate can remove starting-point error faster while leaving more error from noisy updates. Each method balances these two contributions differently. For each batch size, we independently search each method’s stable range for a constant learning rate with the lowest expected loss after all 4,096 samples. Run the small-batch experiment, then try the large-batch preset. The bars show expected final loss, split into remaining initialization error and the noise contribution. The paths show single random runs: at a given batch, SGD and Newton use the same standardized Gaussian draws, scaled by that batch’s noise level.

Change the noise, the curvature, or the starting point and see where the preferred method changes. The paper proves that, for every sufficiently large sample budget, there exists a two-dimensional noisy quadratic where optimally tuned SGD wins at batch 1 and optimally tuned Newton wins when the entire budget forms one batch. The construction also allows optimal momentum tuning. The displayed playground has no momentum and is a separate numerical illustration. Neither result says every landscape must cross over, or that Newton is a stand-in for Shampoo. They establish a possible explanation: the value of a preconditioner can depend on the balance between initialization error and gradient noise. The next question is whether one learning-rate adjustment can balance all directions at once.

Directional scaling rules.

04

Different directions can need different learning-rate scaling.

In SignSGD, a noisy direction benefits from more reliable signs. A direction whose sign is already reliable mostly gets fewer updates.

To examine directional scaling, consider SignSGD with momentum, which steps according to the sign of a momentum-smoothed gradient. In a noisy direction, a larger batch makes the estimated sign more likely to agree with the noiseless gradient’s sign. In a direction whose sign is already reliable, that benefit has nearly saturated; the larger batch mostly gives us fewer updates. The relevant quantity in this illustration is the curvature-to-noise ratio (CNR): curvature squared divided by single-example gradient-noise variance. At the fixed parameter value used here, low CNR means less reliable signs. We scale the learning rate by the batch ratio raised to an exponent α. Here, movement means expected signed displacement toward the minimum per processed sample, relative to batch 1, while holding the parameter fixed. Try to keep both directions at 1×. The two target markers explain why the same exponent cannot generally preserve both.

Where do these curves come from?

Throughout the blog, B is a batch size before scaling, B′ the scaled batch, and κ=B′B their ratio. η is a learning rate, μ a momentum coefficient, and α a learning-rate exponent. Coordinate variables are italic; named optimizer groups are upright subscripts.

Assume independent Gaussian gradient noise. Freeze the coordinate w and let the momentum distribution settle to its steady state; this is stationary momentum. Let μ be the momentum coefficient and κ=B′B the batch ratio. The expected sign is A(B)=erf(wCNR·B(1+μ)2(1−μ)). Relative movement per processed sample is κα−1A(κB)A(B). We use reference B=1. Noise-dominated response grows approximately as B; signal-dominated response saturates. Preserving this local movement motivates square-root or linear scaling in the respective limits. The approximation freezes the parameter and assumes stationary momentum; it does not guarantee full-trajectory invariance. See Section 5 and Appendix C of the paper.

This challenge freezes the parameter and assumes the momentum distribution has settled at that fixed point. It explains local movement, not the learning rate that minimizes an entire training run’s final loss. The paper separately studies independently tuned SignSGD-with-momentum trajectories and finds different scaling behavior across directions. Together, these results make directional heterogeneity worth investigating, but they do not give a universal Muon scaling formula. They also suggest a concrete experiment: when a language model moves to a larger batch, can preserving small-batch updates in a few selected directions remove much of the resulting local loss penalty?

The sharp-subspace intervention.

05

768 sharp directions remove up to 59.5% of the local penalty.

Keep a tiny selected subspace at small batch after a 16× batch increase. A random subspace of the same size changes the penalty by at most 3%.

We branch from intermediate checkpoints of a language model trained with MuonW at 128K tokens per update. In the fully scaled branch, hidden-matrix updates use batches of 2M tokens and square-root learning-rate scaling. In an intervention branch, a selected subspace keeps the small-batch updates while the other directions use large-batch updates. At every base step, we project a small-batch update into that subspace. Every 16 base steps, we also apply the large-batch update projected into its orthogonal complement, using the mean of those 16 gradients and four times their average base learning rate. The selected subspace stays fixed throughout the branch. A control continues with small-batch updates in every direction. Each branch processes the same next 134 million tokens, corresponding to 1,024 base steps. We measure its validation loss above the control and call that difference the local branch penalty.

We select the directions with the largest eigenvalues of a Muon-preconditioned Gauss-Newton curvature proxy, an approximation to how loss bends around the checkpoint after preconditioning. Preserving the sharpest 768 of roughly 85 million hidden-matrix directions reduces the fully scaled branch’s excess validation loss over the small-batch control by up to 59.5%. A random subspace of the same size changes that penalty by at most 3%. The intervention selects directions using curvature; it does not measure their full noise covariance. Use the rank controls to see how the measured recovery changes as more sharp directions are preserved. Then move the training anchor forward: the effect largely disappears near the end of training.

Protocol, controls, and what this does not establish

The held subspace is fixed at each anchor and comes from a Muon-preconditioned Gauss-Newton curvature proxy. Both Muon copies receive the same stream of base-batch gradients; the large-batch copy accumulates 16 of them before an update. Matrix weight decay and auxiliary AdamW updates stay on the base clock. This is a diagnostic intervention, not an ordinary large-batch training run.

Endpoints use 10.5M held-out tokens. Most branches are single runs, so these percentages are descriptive measurements, not significance estimates. The experiment selects directions by curvature and does not measure the full direction-dependent noise covariance. Its effect largely disappears near the end of training. We therefore cannot infer a 59.5% improvement in final loss, total training cost, or wall-clock speed.

06

Sharp-direction recovery fades late in training.

Recovery is substantial at early and middle checkpoints, but close to zero at the final two anchors. This is a local diagnostic; a practical direction-aware scaling method remains open.

The random control matters because it distinguishes the identity of the directions from the number preserved. At early and middle checkpoints, a tiny, deliberately selected subspace recovers much more than an equally sized random one. This supports the view that the local cost of the batch change is unevenly distributed across directions. It does not isolate CNR as the cause, and it does not turn 59.5% local recovery into a 59.5% improvement in final loss or training cost. Most branches are single runs. The experiment also keeps matrix weight decay and auxiliary AdamW updates on the base clock, so it is a diagnostic intervention rather than an ordinary large-batch recipe. Its value is to locate a promising question more precisely: which directional differences should a practical scaling method account for?

Choose the optimizer and batch size together.

A benchmark at one batch size tells us which optimizer worked best there. Our retuned crossovers show why that conclusion should be tested again before carrying it to another batch. For practitioners, the immediate lesson is to compare optimizers at the batches they actually intend to use, with hyperparameters tuned for those settings. Validation loss at a fixed token budget and wall-clock efficiency answer different questions; a useful benchmark should make that distinction clear.

The longer-term question is how to avoid repeating expensive searches. The noisy quadratic shows how geometry and noise can change the value of preconditioning. The language-model intervention shows that a small selected subspace can carry a large part of a local scaling penalty. Connecting those observations to a cheap, reliable direction-aware rule remains open. Batch size belongs in the optimizer-design problem itself.
